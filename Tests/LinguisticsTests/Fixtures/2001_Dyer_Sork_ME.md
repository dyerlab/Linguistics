# Gene flow and population structure in a partially selfing tropical tree, *Swietenia humilis* (Meliaceae)

10.1046/j.1365-294X.2001.01251.x

## Abstract

Understanding gene flow in plant populations requires estimating both pollen- and seed-mediated dispersal. We examined population genetic structure in the tropical dry-forest tree *Swietenia humilis* Zucc. across a fragmented landscape in western Honduras using six microsatellite loci. We combined FST-based methods with Bayesian assignment tests to quantify among-population differentiation and to assess the scale of pollen and seed movement. Significant genetic structure was detected among populations (overall FST = 0.047), but Bayesian clustering identified only weak discrete groupings, suggesting ongoing gene flow among forest fragments. Pollen dispersal distances, inferred from sibship reconstruction, exceeded seed dispersal by an order of magnitude, implicating wind and pollinators in maintaining genetic connectivity across a fragmented landscape.

## Introduction

Habitat fragmentation reduces effective population sizes and isolates previously connected demes, with consequences for genetic diversity, inbreeding, and local adaptation. In tropical dry forests, where deforestation has been particularly severe, many tree species persist as small, isolated remnants embedded in an agricultural matrix. The degree to which remnant populations retain genetic connectivity through ongoing gene flow determines their long-term evolutionary potential.

Gene flow in plants occurs through two vehicles: pollen and seeds. Because pollen typically travels farther than seeds, it is often the primary mechanism maintaining genetic cohesion among populations of outcrossing species. However, the relative contributions of pollen and seed dispersal to overall gene flow remain difficult to disentangle without explicit parentage analysis.

*Swietenia humilis* (Meliaceae), the Pacific-coast mahogany, is a canopy tree of tropical dry forests in Mesoamerica. It is insect pollinated and wind disperses its winged seeds, making it a useful model for studying the relative contributions of the two dispersal modes to gene flow. Widespread logging and agricultural expansion have fragmented its once-continuous range, creating a landscape of small forest remnants separated by open pastureland.

Here we use microsatellite markers and a combination of traditional FST statistics and Bayesian assignment methods to characterize genetic structure among *S. humilis* populations in western Honduras. We test whether fragmentation has disrupted gene flow and quantify the relative roles of pollen and seed dispersal in maintaining among-population connectivity.

## Methods

### Study sites and sampling

Six populations of *S. humilis* were sampled across a 120-km transect in western Honduras. Populations ranged from 18 to 94 adults, with inter-population distances of 15–60 km. Fresh leaves were collected from all adult trees in each population and preserved in silica gel for DNA extraction.

### Microsatellite genotyping

DNA was extracted using a CTAB protocol. Six microsatellite loci (Sh-1, Sh-3, Sh-4, Sh-7, Sh-9, Sh-12) were amplified by PCR and resolved on an ABI 3100 automated sequencer. Allele sizes were scored using GENESCAN 3.7 and GENOTYPER 2.5 (Applied Biosystems).

### Population genetic analyses

Allele frequencies, observed and expected heterozygosity, and departures from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium were estimated using GENEPOP 3.3. Genetic differentiation was quantified by FST (Weir & Cockerham 1984) implemented in FSTAT 2.9.3. Bayesian clustering was performed with STRUCTURE 2.0, running 10 independent replicates for K = 1–10 with 100 000 burn-in and 500 000 MCMC iterations.

Pollen and seed dispersal distances were estimated by Bayesian parentage analysis using CERVUS 3.0. Candidate parents were assigned at 95% confidence, and dispersal distances were calculated as straight-line distances between parent and offspring locations.

## Results

### Genetic diversity

Mean allelic richness across loci ranged from 4.8 (population 3) to 7.1 (population 1). Observed heterozygosity (HO = 0.61–0.71) was consistently below expected heterozygosity (HE = 0.64–0.75), with significant Hardy–Weinberg deficits at two loci in three populations, consistent with partial selfing.

### Population structure

Overall FST was 0.047 (95% CI: 0.031–0.063), indicating moderate but significant genetic differentiation among populations. Pairwise FST values ranged from 0.012 to 0.089. STRUCTURE analyses identified K = 2 as the most likely number of clusters, but individual admixture proportions were high, suggesting diffuse rather than discrete genetic boundaries.

### Dispersal estimates

Parentage analysis assigned fathers to 68% of offspring at 95% confidence. Inferred pollen dispersal distances ranged from 0.3 to 47.8 km (mean 12.4 km), substantially exceeding seed dispersal distances (mean 0.8 km, maximum 3.1 km). Pollen movement across the largest inter-population gaps (>30 km) was detected in three cases, confirming that pollinators cross the agricultural matrix.

## Discussion

Our results indicate that *S. humilis* populations in western Honduras maintain moderate genetic connectivity despite fragmentation, principally through long-distance pollen dispersal. The low but significant FST values and diffuse Bayesian clustering are consistent with ongoing, though reduced, gene flow among remnant populations.

The contrast between pollen and seed dispersal distances is striking. Pollen moved an order of magnitude farther than seeds, a pattern common in wind-pollinated and insect-pollinated tropical trees. The identity of the pollen vectors responsible for the longest-distance movements remains uncertain; hawk moths and large bees are candidates given their known flight ranges.

Partial selfing, evidenced by Hardy–Weinberg deficits, may partly offset the genetic homogenizing effects of pollen flow by increasing within-population relatedness. Conservation strategies for *S. humilis* should prioritize maintaining pollinator communities and connecting forest fragments to sustain both the genetic diversity and the ecological processes that underpin it.
